Most companies choose the truck based on the freight.

The better question is what happens when the truck arrives.

Not every shipment needs a 53' trailer.

<img src="/box-truck-liftgate-delivery.png" alt="Box truck with liftgate delivering palletized freight at a receiver without a loading dock." style="display:block; margin:2.5rem auto; max-width:820px; width:100%; border-radius:1rem;" />

Most equipment decisions start with the shipment. How many pallets, how much weight, how far it's going. Those are reasonable questions, and they're the ones a rate quote is built around. But they describe only half the job.

The other half happens at the destination — and that's usually where deliveries go wrong.

## The freight is simple. The delivery often isn't.

Consider a shipment of six pallets moving a few hundred miles. The freight is palletized, stackable, and doesn't require special handling. Almost any truck could carry it.

Now add the receiver. It's a retail location in a strip center. There's no dock. There's no forklift. The parking lot is shared with customers, and a tractor-trailer can't get turned around in it. Receiving hours run four hours a day and one person works them.

Nothing about the freight changed. Everything about the delivery did.

This is the gap that produces redeliveries, detention, and the phone call nobody wants to make. The equipment was matched to the shipment when it needed to be matched to the site.

## Four questions that should determine the truck

### Does the receiver have a loading dock?

This is the first fork in the road, and it decides more than people expect. Dock-high delivery means the trailer bed and the dock floor meet at roughly the same height, and freight rolls across. No dock means ground-level delivery — the freight has to come *down*, which requires a liftgate, and a liftgate generally means a straight truck.

A dry van at a site with no dock isn't slow. **It's stuck.**

Worth confirming rather than assuming. Plenty of facilities have a dock that's been converted to storage, blocked by a dumpster, or built for a height that doesn't match a standard trailer.

### Who's unloading the freight, and with what?

A dock with a forklift and a dedicated receiving crew is a different delivery than a back door with one employee and a pallet jack.

If there's no forklift on site, someone has to break the pallets down by hand or the driver has to bring the capability with him. That changes the equipment, the time on site, and sometimes the service you should be buying in the first place.

**Ask specifically.** "We can receive freight" and "we can unload a trailer" are not the same sentence.

### How many pallets are actually shipping?

A trailer that's a quarter full costs the same as one that's full. When volume is genuinely small, the flexibility of smaller equipment often outweighs the per-pallet math — especially on time-sensitive freight, where a straight truck going direct beats waiting for a full trailer to fill.

This is also where the LTL assumption creeps in. A handful of pallets looks like an LTL shipment, so it gets tendered that way, and then it moves through two or three terminals to get where it's going. Every terminal transfer is another handling event — and another opportunity for delay or damage.

That's the same idea underneath [Reducing Freight Damage by Eliminating Unnecessary Handoffs](/blog/reducing-freight-damage-eliminating-handoffs/) — every extra time freight changes hands is another chance for something to go wrong.

### Is access restricted for larger equipment?

Turning radius. Overhead clearance. Low bridges on the approach. Alleyway delivery. Downtown loading zones with a time limit. Job sites with soft ground.

A truck that can't physically reach the receiving door hasn't delivered anything, regardless of what the rate was.

## Choosing the right equipment

**Sprinter van.** Small, urgent, and time-critical. One or two pallets or loose freight, fast door-to-door, easy access in tight urban environments. The mistake is using one when the freight genuinely needs a liftgate or exceeds what the van can practically carry — at which point you've bought speed and lost the ability to complete the delivery.

**Box truck with liftgate.** The workhorse for no-dock deliveries. Retail stores, medical offices, small warehouses, job sites, office buildings. A straight truck with liftgate maneuvers where a tractor-trailer can't, and it brings its own unloading capability. For multi-stop routes and final-mile delivery to receivers without docks, it's usually not a compromise — it's the correct answer.

**Dry van.** Right when there's a dock, a forklift, and enough volume to justify the trailer. Wrong when any of those three are missing.

The distinction isn't about size. It's about whether the equipment can complete the delivery unassisted at the far end.

## Why this matters more on some lanes than others

Equipment selection gets harder as receiving conditions get more variable — and few corridors are more variable than the Southeast to the Northeast.

A shipment leaving a Florida or Georgia DC with generous receiving hours and open dock space can arrive at a Northeast receiver with a narrow window, tight urban access, and no room for a 53' trailer.

Same freight. Same shipper. Completely different delivery.

Planning the equipment around the destination — not the origin — is what keeps that from becoming a redelivery.

## The cost of getting it wrong

A truck that can't complete the delivery rarely creates one problem. It creates five.

- Redelivery fees
- Lost transit time
- Freight sitting where it shouldn't
- A receiver forced to adjust their schedule
- A customer asking where their shipment is

Almost none of that shows up in the original rate comparison. All of it shows up later.

## The short version

The right truck isn't determined by what's being shipped.

It's determined by what happens when the truck arrives.

Getting that question answered before pickup takes a few minutes. Solving the wrong equipment after delivery takes considerably longer.
