# Partial Truckload vs. LTL: When Direct Freight Makes More Sense
**The Freight That Doesn’t Fit the Plan**

Every shipper I talk to already has carriers they like. I’m not trying to take that business away from people who are doing a good job.

I want the other call.

The 4,000-pound order that has to be there Thursday, but the LTL quote came back at five days. The shipment that’s too big to parcel out, too small to fill a trailer, and becomes the hardest freight in the building to move.

That’s the freight I want.

## The Freight That Gets Punished
The problem usually lives in the middle — a few pallets, a few thousand pounds, and nowhere near enough freight to fill a trailer.

It’s too large or too urgent for parcel. LTL will move it, but the shipment gets picked up, taken to a terminal, unloaded, sorted, loaded again, transferred to another terminal and handled all over again.

That works fine when the freight has time.

When it has to be there Thursday, five days doesn’t help you.

Every extra handling point creates another chance for something to go wrong. A pallet gets crushed. Cases get separated. Freight gets loaded on the wrong trailer. An appointment gets missed.

Then somebody asks where the shipment was on Tuesday.

Nobody really knows.

## Price the Direct Move Before You Accept the Transit
This is the part I see shippers overlook all the time.

They get an LTL quote, see that it’s cheaper than a full truckload, and stop there.

But full truckload isn’t the only alternative.

A 4,000-pound shipment doesn’t need 53 feet of trailer. Depending on the freight and the lane, it can move on a box truck, partial truckload or expedited equipment.

Price that option before accepting five days of transit.

A lot of the time, the difference between LTL and moving the freight direct isn’t nearly as big as people assume. On some shipments, it’s basically the same money.

Except now the freight gets picked up once and delivered once.

No terminals. No transfers. No three-day mystery in the middle.

It’s on the truck.

That doesn’t mean every LTL shipment belongs on dedicated equipment. It means the comparison is worth making when the shipment is time-sensitive, fragile, expensive, difficult to replace or important to the customer waiting on the other end.

Saving $150 on transportation doesn’t mean much if the freight arrives two days late and somebody has to explain why production stopped.
