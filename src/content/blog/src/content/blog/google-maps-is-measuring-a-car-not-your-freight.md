One of the most common conversations we have with customers starts the same way:

*"Google says it's an eight-hour drive."*

Someone pulls up the route, sees eight hours, and sets the appointment accordingly.

That number isn't wrong. It just isn't describing a truck.

Google Maps is measuring how long a passenger car takes to get from Point A to Point B. We're planning a commercial vehicle moving tens of thousands of pounds of freight while complying with federal regulations, equipment restrictions, and the realities of the transportation industry.

Those aren't the same trip. Here's what happens between the shipper's dock and the receiver's that the map has no way to know about.

## The trip starts well before the first mile

Most people picture a truck pulling off the dock the moment loading is complete. In reality, loading is often just the beginning.

After the trailer is loaded, the driver heads to a certified truck scale. Federal weight limits apply not just to total gross weight, but to how that weight sits on each axle group. A load can be perfectly legal at 79,000 pounds gross and still be illegal because too much of it landed over the drives.

If the axle weights are off, the driver slides the tandems and scales again.

Sometimes once. Sometimes twice.

That's not a delay. It's a normal, legal part of hauling freight — and depending on the truck stop and the line at the scale, it can take an hour before the truck has moved a mile toward the receiver.

## Trucks don't get the same roads

Google Maps assumes you're driving a car. Commercial trucks don't have that luxury.

Low bridges. Weight-restricted roads. Parkways that prohibit commercial vehicles entirely. Local truck bans, hazmat routing rules, and residential streets a 53-foot trailer physically cannot navigate.

The shortest route for a passenger vehicle is frequently illegal for a tractor-trailer. The legal truck route is often longer than the one on the screen — not because the carrier chose the scenic version, but because it's the only one he can legally drive.

Those extra miles are real miles. They show up in the transit time whether or not the appointment accounted for them.

## Every stop costs more than you'd expect

Fueling a truck isn't the fill-up. It's the line for the pump.

Drivers wait behind several other trucks before reaching an open lane. At the wrong stop at the wrong hour, that's 30 minutes before the truck even reaches the pump. Then there's the fuel itself — hundreds of gallons doesn't flow as fast as a fifteen-gallon fill-up.

Then there are weigh stations.

A driver may get waved through in seconds. Or he may get pulled in.

A full inspection — a Level I, the most thorough of the six — is 45 minutes to an hour, and he doesn't get a vote. Neither does the carrier. It happens on the enforcement officer's schedule, not the delivery appointment's.

## The clock runs differently

Commercial drivers operate under federal Hours of Service regulations, and these rules govern the trip far more than traffic does.

A driver can drive a maximum of 11 hours, but only within a 14-hour window that starts the moment he goes on duty. That window doesn't pause for a slow dock, a long scale line, or a fuel stop. Once it's running, it's running.

There's also a required 30-minute break after eight hours of driving. Federal, not optional. Not something a driver can skip because an appointment is running behind — and it's logged electronically, so there's no discretion in it.

And when the window closes, the driver needs 10 consecutive hours off duty before he can legally drive again.

On top of the regulations, many fleets govern their trucks below the posted limit for safety and fuel economy. Every hour on the map stretches a little longer in the real world. Over 500 miles, a five-mile-per-hour difference is roughly an extra hour — with perfect traffic.

## Then there's the dock on the other end

Transit time gets all the attention, but the appointment itself is part of the equation.

Receivers have their own schedules, their own door capacity, and their own priorities. A truck that arrives at 2:00 PM for a 2:00 PM appointment may still sit two hours before a door opens. If the driver's 14-hour clock expires while he's sitting in that yard, the load doesn't move again until the next day — no matter how well the first 400 miles went.

Detention isn't a hypothetical. It's one of the most common reasons an on-time pickup becomes a late delivery.

## Stack it all together

Scaling. Truck routing. Fuel. Weigh stations. Mandatory breaks. Governed speeds. Dock time.

An eight-hour drive becomes a ten- or eleven-hour day.

Nothing went wrong. There wasn't an accident. The weather was perfect.

That's the good version.

## Why experienced transportation professionals build cushion

The problem isn't any one of these. It's that an appointment built off a car's transit time has zero margin.

The first unexpected event — a longer fuel line, a weigh station inspection, heavy dock traffic — is enough to make the load late. And once a load is late, the recovery options get expensive fast.

Experienced transportation professionals build cushion into transit times. Not because they're being conservative. Because they've watched what a day on the road actually costs.

They don't build schedules around the fastest possible trip. They build them around the most likely one.

That's why we ask questions before committing to pickup and delivery appointments. What are the dock hours? Is the receiver appointment-only? How much dwell time is typical at that facility? Is there a hard cutoff, or is there flexibility on the back end?

We're not trying to make the schedule longer. We're trying to make it achievable.

Because the best transportation plan isn't the one that looks best on paper. It's the one that still works when real life gets involved.

When we tell you a transit time doesn't work, we're not negotiating.

We're reading a different map than you are.
