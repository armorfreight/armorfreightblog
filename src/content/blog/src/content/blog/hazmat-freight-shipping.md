## Why Hazmat Changes the Rate

There is no universal Armor hazmat surcharge.

The rate changes because the number of trucks that can actually handle the shipment changes.

Consider a simplified dry-van search.

### Standard Freight

- 20 possible carriers
  - 10 are positioned correctly
  - 6 have the right equipment and schedule
  - 4 want the lane at a workable rate
  - 3 can meet the pickup and delivery requirements
  - **3 realistic options**

Now run the same lane with placarded hazmat.

### Placarded Hazmat

- 20 possible carriers
  - 10 are positioned correctly
  - 6 have the right equipment and schedule
  - 3 meet the hazmat requirements and accept the commodity
  - 2 want the lane at a workable rate
  - 1 can meet the pickup and delivery requirements
  - **1 realistic option**

The origin did not change. The destination did not change. The trailer did not change.

The usable carrier pool did.

If 20 trucks are showing in the market but only two can realistically execute your shipment, those two trucks are the market for that load.

Armor also does not count every available truck as usable capacity. Through the Armstrong network, carriers must be properly set up and approved before they are available for freight booking. That includes active operating authority, minimum insurance requirements, and a signed carrier agreement.

Armstrong's published carrier requirements include at least **$100,000 in cargo insurance and $1 million in liability coverage** before a carrier is approved into the network.

From there, the carrier still has to match the equipment, timing, commodity, and specific requirements of the hazmat shipment.

Now add an expedited pickup.

Run the same funnel again, but put a **90-minute clock** on it. That one remaining option can quickly become **0–1 trucks that can physically reach the shipper in time**.

When that happens, Armor starts working the constraints. We can widen the search radius, look at alternate qualified equipment, test whether the pickup window has flexibility, compare dedicated and partial options where appropriate, and determine which requirement is actually controlling the shipment.

Sometimes there is one truck. Sometimes the pickup time needs to move. Sometimes different equipment opens another option.

The goal is to identify that early enough for the shipper to make a decision instead of going quiet while the clock runs.

## A Small Load Can Still Be a Placarded Load

Physical size can be misleading.

Under federal placarding rules, **Table 1 materials generally require placards at any quantity**. For most non-bulk **Table 2 materials**, the familiar highway threshold is **1,001 pounds aggregate gross weight**, subject to the applicable exceptions and special rules.

On a box truck or straight truck, 1,001 pounds arrives quickly.

A few drums or cylinders can occupy very little floor space while still creating placarding and driver-qualification requirements that narrow the available equipment pool.

That is why Armor does not assume a physically small hazmat shipment belongs on the smallest available truck. We look at the commodity and quantity first, then determine whether a sprinter, box truck, straight truck, partial truckload option, or dedicated dry van makes sense.

## Compressed Gas: Four Questions That Change the Move

Compressed-gas shipments are a good example of why Armor asks more than pallet count and weight.

**What is actually in the cylinders?**
“Oxygen tanks” or “gas cylinders” is not enough. The proper shipping description and hazards determine what we are moving and can affect the transportation options.

**Are they full, partially full, or empty?**
Empty does not automatically mean non-hazmat. Cylinders containing hazardous-material residue may still need to move as hazardous material unless an applicable exception has been met.

**How are they packaged and secured?**
Cylinder size, count, racks, pallets, valve protection, and securement can change which equipment is practical.

**What else would be riding on the truck?**
This matters when a shipper wants to consolidate a smaller cylinder shipment with other freight.

Compressed oxygen, for example, is non-flammable but presents an oxidizing hazard. Depending on the other freight, compatibility or segregation concerns can change whether shared capacity is practical.

A shipment that initially looks like a good partial-load candidate may ultimately make more sense on dedicated equipment.

That is why we ask the questions before committing a truck.

## What Armor Checks Before Dispatch

Most hazmat paperwork problems are easier to address while we are building the shipment than after the driver is sitting at the dock.

Before dispatch, Armor looks for transportation details that could create a stop at pickup. Does the shipping description match what we are being asked to move? Will required placards be available? Is the shipper certification complete where required? Is the emergency-response contact usable while the freight is in transportation?

We are not replacing the shipper's hazmat compliance program or certifying the shipment on the shipper's behalf.

We are trying to catch transportation problems early.

Because when they are not caught, the escalation is familiar: the driver calls dispatch, dispatch calls Armor, Armor calls the shipper, the warehouse looks for the shipping manager, someone finds EHS, and a corrected document has to be produced.

Meanwhile, the truck is still sitting.

That is how a small paperwork issue becomes detention, a missed appointment, or a driver-hours problem.

Catching it before dispatch is cheaper for everyone.

## Hazmat Can Change the Route Too

Carrier availability is only part of the lead-time equation.

The legal route can change as well.

That matters on corridors Armor regularly coordinates between **Florida, the Mid-Atlantic, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and New York**.

Consider freight moving north from Jacksonville toward the Lehigh Valley or New York metro area.

Certain hazardous materials face restrictions through the **Fort McHenry Tunnel on I-95** and the **Baltimore Harbor Tunnel on I-895**. Depending on the material and quantity, a driver may need to route around the normal Baltimore crossing.

That can add miles and drive time and can determine whether the original delivery appointment still works.

Farther north, hazardous-material restrictions also affect crossings including the **Holland Tunnel, Lincoln Tunnel, and portions of the George Washington Bridge**.

So the question is not always simply:

**How many miles is Jacksonville from the consignee?**

Sometimes it is:

**What route can this truck, carrying this material in this quantity, legally use to get there?**

A standard mapping program may show one ETA. The usable hazmat route can produce another.

That is something Armor wants to identify before the rate and appointment are committed.
