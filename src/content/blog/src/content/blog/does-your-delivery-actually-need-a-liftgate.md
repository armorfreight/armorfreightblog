A liftgate sounds like a simple accessorial.

Either the receiver needs one or they don't.

In reality, liftgate deliveries are one of those freight details that seem minor during quoting and become a very big problem when the truck is sitting at the receiver with a pallet that nobody can unload.

The issue usually isn't the liftgate itself. It's that nobody asked enough questions before the shipment was booked.

## A Pallet Does Not Automatically Mean You Need a Liftgate
A liftgate is used when freight needs to move between the truck and ground level and there isn't a dock, forklift, or other suitable equipment available to unload it.

If the receiver has a loading dock the truck can back into, a liftgate usually isn't necessary.

If there's a forklift on site, along with enough room to safely access and unload the truck, you may not need one either.

But if there's no dock, no forklift, and a pallet has to come off the truck at ground level, now we're talking about a liftgate delivery.

That distinction matters because simply saying, "It's palletized freight," doesn't tell a carrier enough about what will happen when the truck arrives.

## Getting It Off the Truck Is Only Half the Question
Here's where many shipments get complicated.

Getting the freight off the truck is not necessarily the same thing as getting it where the customer wants it.

A liftgate solves one specific problem: moving the freight between trailer height and ground level.

It does not automatically mean the driver is taking that pallet through a doorway, across a parking lot, down a hallway, into a stockroom, around the back of a building, or to a specific location inside the facility.

Those are additional delivery requirements.

This becomes especially important with retail locations, offices, job sites, medical facilities, smaller warehouses, residential deliveries, and businesses that were never designed to receive commercial freight.

A shipper might tell you, "They just need a liftgate."

The receiver might be expecting the driver to lower the pallet, move it 150 feet, get it through a narrow door, and place it inside a storage room.

Those are two very different deliveries.

And they're going to be quoted differently.

## The Questions That Should Be Asked Before Pickup
Before I book a shipment requiring a liftgate or specialized delivery, I want to understand exactly what the driver is going to encounter.

At minimum:

- Is there a receiving dock?
- Is a forklift available?
- How heavy is the pallet?
- Will the freight physically fit on the liftgate platform?
- Is the liftgate rated to safely handle it?
- Is there enough room to operate a pallet jack?
- Is the delivery curbside or inside?
- Are there stairs, gates, narrow doors, gravel, slopes, or other access issues?
- Where does the receiver actually expect the freight to be placed?

These aren't unnecessary questions.

They're the difference between planning a delivery and hoping the driver figures it out when they get there.

## Weight Isn't the Only Limitation
A liftgate isn't unlimited capacity.

The weight of the shipment matters, but so does its footprint.

A pallet can be within the gate's weight capacity and still be too long, wide, awkward, or unstable to safely lower on the platform.

Oversized machinery, long crates, unusually shaped freight, top-heavy pallets, and freight with an uneven center of gravity all deserve a closer look before somebody simply checks the "liftgate required" box.

The right question isn't just:

**How much does it weigh?**

It's:

**Will this specific freight safely fit on the equipment we're sending?**

That's a much better question to answer before the truck arrives.

## Inside Delivery Is Not the Same as Liftgate Delivery
This distinction is worth repeating because it's probably the most common misunderstanding.

**Liftgate delivery gets the freight between the truck and the ground.**

**Inside delivery means the freight has to travel beyond that point.**

Depending on the shipment, that may require additional labor, equipment, time, or even a different type of carrier altogether.

If the receiver expects freight inside a building, make that clear before the load is booked.

"Liftgate required" is not a substitute for complete delivery instructions.

## Five Minutes Now Can Save Hours Later
A lot of freight problems aren't really transportation problems.

They're information problems.

A driver arrives.

There's no dock.

The consignee doesn't have a forklift.

The freight weighs 2,000 pounds.

The receiver thought the driver was bringing it inside.

And suddenly everybody is calling everybody trying to figure out what happens next.

That situation was avoidable.

Five minutes of questions before pickup could have identified the problem before there was a truck sitting in the parking lot.

That's why we try to understand the entire delivery before matching the equipment to the shipment.

Not just where the freight is going.

How it's coming off the truck.

Where it's going after that.

And what the driver is actually expected to do when they arrive.

## Know the Unload Before You Book the Load
Transportation doesn't end when the truck reaches the destination.

A successful delivery means the equipment, freight, access requirements, and receiver expectations all line up.

So before worrying about how the freight is getting there, make sure you know how it's getting off the truck.
