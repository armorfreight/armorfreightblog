Too many times in this business you hear about a truck “breaking down” on the way to pick up the freight.

That very seldom happens in our operation, and it’s not luck. It’s because I understand what it actually costs to run a truck and what it takes to keep a carrier on the load once they’ve booked it.

The short version: when a load gets underbid, the carrier doesn’t turn it down — he takes it as a placeholder while he keeps looking. When something better comes along, the truck “breaks down.” The rate didn’t just buy you a price. It bought you the odds that the truck shows up.

## What a carrier breakdown before pickup usually means

Here’s what actually happened on most of those loads: they got underbid.

The carrier took it anyway — not as a commitment, but as a placeholder. Something on the board in case nothing better came along. The rate wasn’t good, but it was better than sitting empty, so he covered it and kept looking. Then a load pops up paying a few hundred more, and suddenly there’s a mechanical issue.

The thing about a mechanical issue is that nobody can argue with it. You can’t inspect it over the phone. You can’t ask for pictures of a wheel seal at six in the morning. The load is gone, the driver’s on something else, and you’re the one making calls.

That’s not most carriers. Plenty of them will honor a cheap load because that’s who they are and they said they would. But you’re not pricing for the carrier who’ll eat it. You’re pricing for the one who has a choice on Tuesday morning — and a rate at the absolute bottom gives him almost no reason to choose you.

## What an underbid load actually costs

You can win the quote and still not have a truck.

Underbidding a lane doesn’t remove risk. It moves it. You saved a few hundred dollars on paper, and now the exposure sits somewhere else:

- The missed appointment, and whatever the receiver does about it
- The recovery truck, booked same-day, at whatever the market says it’s worth
- Detention on the replacement, because the dock is backed up by the time he gets there
- The lost production time on the receiving end, which nobody invoices but everybody pays
- The call to the customer at 4:30 that nobody wants to make

Not one of those shows up on the rate confirmation. All of them show up in what the load actually cost.

The math on a recovery truck is the part nobody prices in. You don’t buy a replacement at the same rate as the original load. You buy it at whatever the market charges when you need it same-day, in a lane you’ve already lost time on, and with the appointment already blowing up. That truck costs what somebody willing to take the call can charge you, and carriers know exactly how much leverage you have when you’re trying to get freight moved before lunch.

So the few hundred you saved by underbidding the original tender gets spent before nine in the morning. Then you pay detention on the replacement, you absorb the missed appointment, and you still have the receiver sitting on a line they can’t run.

You can only beat a carrier down so far before your load stops being a commitment.

## Is a cheap freight rate ever the right call?

Sometimes, and it’s worth being honest about when.

If the freight is flexible, the lane is dense, the pickup window is wide and nothing downstream breaks if it delivers a day late, then buying the cheapest available truck is a reasonable decision. Plenty of freight fits that description.

The trouble is that most shippers price all their freight that way, including the loads where it doesn’t fit — the tight appointment, the hazmat, the single-source part that shuts a line down, the customer who’s already on you about the last one. Those loads need a truck that stays, and a bottom-dollar rate is the least reliable way to get one.

## What we do instead

I’m not trying to overpay anybody. I want to be clear about that, because “pay carriers more” is easy to say and it isn’t the argument.

The argument is that the rate is part of execution. I’m paying a number where the carrier actually wants to keep the load — where, when something better comes across the board, he still has a reason to run mine. That’s a commercial decision, not a generous one. It’s the cheapest way I know to buy a truck that shows up.

The rate is one piece of it. Before we call a load covered, we’re also confirming:

- The driver has the hours to make the pickup and the delivery
- The equipment is right for the freight, not just close enough
- The pickup and delivery requirements are confirmed, not assumed
- We can reach the person actually driving the truck if something changes

All of that is worth less than it should be if the number underneath it gives him a reason to leave.

## Everybody in the deal is trying to make money

The customer. The people selling the freight. The guy actually driving the truck.

There’s nothing wrong with any of that. It’s how the business works. The problem starts when somebody prices a lane as if one of those three doesn’t need to make anything on it — because that person doesn’t disappear from the transaction. He just stops being reliable.

And if somebody is dramatically under market, that difference is coming out of somewhere. The carrier absorbs it today. The provider absorbs it tomorrow. The customer absorbs it the day the load falls apart.

If you’ve taken one of those breakdown calls in the last few months, you already know what it cost you — and it wasn’t the rate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do carriers cancel loads after they’ve already booked them?**  
Most often because the rate was low enough that the load was never a firm commitment. The carrier books it as a fallback while he keeps shopping, and drops it when better-paying freight appears. Equipment failures are real and do happen, but a cancellation that arrives right as the market tightens is usually a pricing outcome, not a maintenance one.

**How can you tell a real breakdown from an excuse?**  
You often can’t prove it, which is exactly why it’s the excuse of choice. What you can look at is the pattern: whether it happened on a load priced below market, whether the driver went quiet before the call came, and whether the same carrier has done it before. A carrier with a genuine failure will usually stay on the phone and try to help you recover.

**Does paying a higher rate guarantee the carrier shows up?**  
No. A fair rate doesn’t guarantee service — vetting, hours, equipment and communication all still matter. What it does is remove the incentive to drop your load for a better one, which is the single most common reason capacity falls off before pickup.

**What should I do when a carrier falls off a load?**  
Get the cancellation confirmed in writing, notify the shipper and receiver immediately with a realistic ETA on a replacement, work known carriers before going to the open market, and post at a real rate rather than a fishing rate. The first thirty minutes decide how expensive the day gets.

**Why is one quote hundreds of dollars below the others?**  
Because someone in the chain is absorbing the difference. That’s the carrier’s margin, the provider’s margin, or — if the load falls apart — the customer’s schedule. A quote that’s far under the rest of the market is priced on the assumption that nothing goes wrong.
